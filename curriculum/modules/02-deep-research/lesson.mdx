# Deep research

## In plain words

- **Goal:** Build a small fact pack before you write rules, prompts, or tools.
- **Do this:** Ask three narrow questions. Save every link with a date. Write it up as BRIEF.md and SOURCES.md.
- **You'll know it worked:** A teammate can open your files and continue without your chat history.

## What this is

**Deep research** means fact-finding before you start. You gather real pages and notes first. Then you build rules, prompts, and tools on top of facts.

Think of it like homework before a group project. You read the textbook. You save links. You write a one-page summary. You do not start building from memory or guesses.

The output is a small **research pack** — files your team can reuse. At minimum you need a one-page project summary (**BRIEF.md**) and a proof list with links and dates (**SOURCES.md**). A shared word list (**GLOSSARY.md**) helps when terms get confusing.

This is not the same as chatting. Chatting asks the helper to remember or invent. Deep research **collects** evidence from the real world.

## Why it matters

Sam started a new AI project by chatting. The helper sounded confident. It invented product names, pricing, and API steps that did not exist. Sam wrote wall rules and **playbooks** — recipe cards for one kind of job — from those guesses. Three weeks later, nothing matched the real docs. The team had to throw away work and start over.

A two-hour research pack would have stopped that. Sam would have had links, dates, and a clear goal tied to real sources. Every later step works better when facts come first.

## Big ideas

### Research is not chatting

In a chat, the model may blend old training with your words. It can sound sure when it is wrong.

Deep research **collects** pages, docs, and notes you can check. Treat every scrape as draft evidence. You still verify important claims before you ship.

**Design rule:** If a claim matters for a rule or a build, give it a source line in SOURCES.md first. Only then does it belong in BRIEF.md.

### Your project pack

Make simple files you can attach later. Keep them short. Do not paste a whole website into classroom wall rules.

| File | What it holds |
|------|----------------|
| BRIEF.md | One-page summary: goal, users, limits, open questions |
| SOURCES.md | URL, date, and one line on why each link matters |
| GLOSSARY.md | Shared words your team must use the same way (optional) |

BRIEF.md is the map. SOURCES.md is the proof drawer. Later lessons will look up facts in these files instead of guessing.

### Browse and crawl tools

A **crawl tool** helps you find pages, copy clean text, and save what you found. Many products offer browse, search, or deep-research modes. The habit matters more than the brand name.

Pick a tool that lets you save URL plus date for every key page. Raw scrapes can live in a scrap folder. Attach BRIEF.md to the desk — not the whole crawl.

**Design rule:** Research tools belong in a research workflow. You do not need them open for every short draft.

### Clear goals need facts

A vague goal gives vague builds.

Bad goal: "Build an AI helper for sales."

Better goal: "Help SDRs prep first calls. Use facts from the clinic website. No medical claims. Cite SOURCES.md for pricing."

The second goal names users, limits, and where proof lives. That makes verify easy later.

### A simple walkthrough

You need to build standing rules for a customer-support bot.

1. **Scope** — Write three narrow questions. Example: "What does the official refund policy say?"
2. **Collect** — Use a browse or crawl tool to save five first-hand pages. Note URL and date for each.
3. **Pack** — Write BRIEF.md with goal, users, and out-of-scope lines. List every big claim with a source pointer.
4. **SOURCES.md** — One row per link: URL, date, why it matters.
5. **Handoff** — A teammate opens BRIEF.md and can continue without your chat history.

If step 5 fails, your pack is too vague or missing links. Fix the pack before you write wall rules.

## Where this sits in the loop

The mental model lesson gave you the full context loop map. Deep research is **fact-finding before you start** — the step that fills your binder before anything else loads onto the desk.

It sits right after the map and right before always-on behavior rules. Your research pack becomes the fact base for wall rules and binder lookup later. Skip research and those steps encode folklore instead of proof.

## What goes wrong if you skip it

- Rules and playbooks encode guesses. They sound official but match no real doc.
- Teams debate "what the AI said" instead of checking a link.
- Tool setups aim at APIs and pages that do not exist.
- You paste huge crawls onto the desk and bury your actual question.
- Verify fails late because nobody wrote down what "done" meant or where proof lives.

## Tips

1. **Ask three narrow questions**, not "research everything about X."
2. **Save URL plus date** for every key claim. Pages change.
3. **Keep raw scrapes in a scrap folder.** Attach BRIEF.md later — not the whole crawl.
4. **Time-box research.** Two focused hours beat two vague days.
5. **List open questions** in BRIEF.md so binder lookup knows what is still missing.
6. **Update the pack when facts change.** Stale SOURCES.md is worse than no source at all.
