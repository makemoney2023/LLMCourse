# Human craft

## What this is

**Human craft** is how you aim the helper. You write a clear ask. You keep one job at a time. You choose modes. You approve risky moves before they run.

Helpers are eager. A fuzzy ask still gets a polished answer — aimed wrong. Your craft is the steering wheel on the context loop.

This lesson covers short specs, reverse prompting, and approval gates. It connects your intent to everything on the desk.

## Why it matters

Jordan typed: "Make this better." The helper rewrote a legal paragraph as marketing fluff. Jordan was upset. The ask had no audience, no limits, and no done checks.

A six-line spec would have fixed it. Outcome: plain summary for customers. Limits: no new claims. Sources: BRIEF.md. Done: three bullet points under 100 words.

Craft is not "prompt tricks." It is clear intent so verify has something to check.

## Big ideas

### Short specs win

You do not need a novel. You need clarity. A good spec fits on one screen:

- **Outcome** — what artifact you want
- **Audience** — who will read or use it
- **Limits** — what not to do
- **Sources** — BRIEF.md, SOURCES.md, or named files
- **Done checks** — how you will know it is right

**Design rule:** If a teammate could run your spec without asking three follow-up questions, it is strong enough.

### One job per turn

Stacking ten asks in one message drops later asks. The model may do the first two well and skim the rest.

Finish one job. Verify lightly. Then send the next intent. Mixed jobs pollute the desk the same way mixed topics do in Module 7.

### Reverse questioning

When the ask is fuzzy, flip the order. Tell the helper: "Ask questions first. Do not draft yet."

Answer the questions. Then allow the draft. This is **reverse prompting** — you let the helper pull clarity from you before it spends tokens guessing.

Use it when scope is unclear, stakes are high, or you feel stuck on wording.

### Modes and approvals

**Plan mode** (or "questions only") fits unclear scope and high risk. The helper maps steps before acting.

**Go mode** fits clear checklist work with low blast radius.

Require **approval** before:

- Sending email or messages to real people
- Deleting files or data
- Paying, purchasing, or changing billing
- Production deploys and live config changes

Rubber-stamp approvals are worse than no gate — they train you to ignore warnings.

### A simple walkthrough

You need a status note for your manager.

1. **Spec** — Outcome: five-bullet weekly status. Audience: manager. Limits: no invented metrics. Sources: ticket links in BRIEF.md. Done: each bullet cites a ticket or says "blocked."
2. **Mode** — Clear scope → go mode. If metrics were unclear → plan mode first.
3. **One job** — Ask for status only. Do not also ask for slide design in the same turn.
4. **Run** — Helper drafts. Tool results stay small.
5. **Verify** — Module 11 checklist: done checks, sources, no secrets, no extra edits.
6. **Next intent** — If slides are next, compact or restart with a fresh handoff.

If step 5 fails, fix the spec — not the model brand.

Craft sits at the front of every turn. Bad intent wastes good wall rules, tools, and memory.

## What goes wrong if you skip it

- Fast wrong work — polished but mis-aimed.
- Rubber-stamp approvals — risky sends slip through.
- Blame aimed at "the model" when the spec was vague.
- Ten asks in one message — later jobs get skimmed.
- Plan mode skipped on high-risk jobs — no shared map.
- Verify fails because done checks were never written.

## Tips

1. **Keep an eight-line spec template** in a recipe card (Module 4).
2. **End every spec with "Done when:"** and three checks.
3. **Separate explore chats from execute chats** — research vs ship.
4. **Bring SOURCES.md when facts matter** — do not rely on memory alone.
5. **Use reverse prompting** when you feel vague — force questions first.
6. **Match mode to risk** — plan when blast radius is high.

You steer the loop. The helper accelerates — it does not choose the destination.
